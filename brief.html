Inspecting Without Examples

Catching defective parts when you have no pictures of defects to learn from — and why the accuracy number is not the number that matters.

The short version

  • A production line cannot supply training examples of defects. By the time you have collected five hundred of them, you have shipped five hundred of them.
  • So the model learns only what good looks like, and flags anything that does not fit. No defect examples required.
  • The model does not output pass or fail. It outputs a score. Somebody still has to decide where the line goes.
  • That decision is a business decision, not a modelling one — because a missed defect and a false alarm have very different prices.
  • The published accuracy figures for this task (~99%) do not tell you how many defects would reach a customer. A different number does, and it is the one this work reports.

The problem with normal machine learning here

Almost every model you have heard about learns from examples of the thing it is looking for. Show it ten thousand photographs labelled cat and ten thousand labelled dog, and it learns the difference.

Visual quality inspection breaks that arrangement. A well-run line produces overwhelmingly good parts — that is the entire point of a well-run line. Defects are rare, and they are also varied: a scratch, a crack, a misprint, a missing component, and a contamination are five different-looking problems that share nothing except being wrong.

Two consequences follow, and both are awkward. You cannot gather a balanced training set without deliberately producing scrap. And even if you gathered one, it would only cover the defect types you had already seen — the model would be blind to the sixth failure mode that shows up next quarter when a supplier changes material.

The reframing Rather than learning what defects look like, learn what normal looks like — and treat anything sufficiently far from normal as suspect. Good parts you have in abundance, for free, already.

How it actually works

The system builds a reference of normality from defect-free parts alone, then measures how far each new part sits from that reference.

The feature extractor is a standard pretrained vision model, used as-is. It is not trained on your parts; it simply converts an image into a compact numerical description. Two visually similar parts land close together in that numerical space, and a scratched part lands further away from everything in the reference.

The important structural point for planning purposes: there is no training run. Setting this up on a new product line means photographing good parts and building the reference, not commissioning a training job. That has real consequences for how quickly a new line can be brought online.

Where the decision actually gets made

Here is the part that gets lost when this work is summarised as an accuracy percentage.

The model produces a number, not a verdict. Good parts tend to score low and defective parts tend to score high — but the two ranges overlap. Some unusual-but-acceptable parts score higher than some subtly defective ones. No threshold separates them cleanly, because no such threshold exists.

So somebody chooses where the line goes, and that choice determines everything the business experiences. Move it right and fewer good parts get pulled for review, but more defects reach customers. Move it left and you catch more defects while stopping more good parts. Try it:

Notice that there is no setting where both numbers are zero. That is not a deficiency in the model — it is the shape of the problem. The overlap is real, and the only question is which side of it you would rather absorb.

Why the published accuracy figure does not answer the question

Methods for this task are compared using a metric called AUROC, usually quoted around 99% on the standard benchmark. It is a fair metric and it is widely misread, so it is worth one paragraph.

AUROC measures ranking, not decisions. It asks: if you drew one good part and one defective part at random, how often would the defective one score higher? At 99%, almost always. What it never mentions is where the threshold sits — because it averages over all possible thresholds. Two systems with identical AUROC can, at the threshold you actually deploy, differ substantially in how many defects reach customers.

So a 99% AUROC is a genuine statement about a model's ranking quality and tells a plant manager nothing about escapes per shift. The numbers that do are the ones in the panel above: escapes, false alarms, and what they cost together.

Why this matters commercially A supplier quoting "99% accurate" has not told you their escape rate, and may not have measured it. Asking for escapes and false alarms at a stated operating point is the question that separates a real evaluation from a benchmark score.

Calibrating blind

There is a subtle trap in choosing the threshold, and it is worth understanding because it is easy to get wrong in a way that looks fine.

The obvious approach is to try thresholds until escapes and false alarms are nicely balanced. But doing that requires a pile of known defects to measure against — and the entire premise of this method is that you do not have one. At commissioning time on a real line, you have good parts and nothing else.

So the threshold has to be set from good parts alone: run the reference set through the system, look at the distribution of scores that known-good parts produce, and place the line where an acceptable fraction of them would be flagged. Set it at the 99th percentile and roughly one good part in a hundred goes for review — a false alarm rate you have chosen deliberately and can staff for.

A threshold tuned against a test set that contains defects will report better numbers and will not reproduce them in production. This work sets it the honest way, from defect-free data only, which is why the reported figures are lower and are the ones worth trusting.

Not every product is equally inspectable

The benchmark covers fifteen product categories, and their difficulty varies enormously. Reporting a single averaged figure across all of them conceals that completely — which is why this work reports every category separately.

CategoryTypeWhy it behaves as it does
bottleObjectConsistent shape and framing, visible defects. Close to solved.
screwObjectParts sit at arbitrary rotations and defects are a few pixels wide. Persistently the hardest.
carpetTextureNo fixed structure at all; the model must learn a statistical pattern rather than a shape.

The practical read for a manager: feasibility is per-product, not per-technology. A pilot that succeeds on one component says little about the next one, and the difference is usually defect size relative to the frame, plus how consistently the part is presented to the camera. Both are fixable with fixturing and optics, which is often cheaper than any modelling improvement.

What this does not do

Three limits worth stating plainly, since they shape what a deployment would need.

It flags, it does not diagnose. The output is "this part is unusual", not "this part has a 4 mm crack in the flange". Defect classification is a separate problem needing labelled defect examples — the very thing this approach avoids.

It assumes the reference stays valid. Change the material, the lighting, the camera position, or the supplier, and yesterday's definition of normal is wrong. Everything starts reading as anomalous. Re-referencing is cheap, but it has to be triggered by somebody, and drift monitoring is a real part of the work.

It inherits its notion of similarity from a general-purpose model. The feature extractor was trained on everyday photographs, not your parts. Where a defect is visually subtle in ways that ordinary photography never cared about, the extractor may simply not represent it.

What comes next

The work is staged so each step answers a question the previous one raised.

StageQuestion it answers
1 — baselineHow far does the simplest possible method get, and what does it cost at a threshold set the honest way? Everything later must beat this.
2 — published methodsDo the more sophisticated published methods actually improve the numbers a plant cares about, or only the benchmark score?
3 — data efficiencyHow many good images are needed before performance plateaus? This sets the real commissioning cost for a new line.

Stage three is the one with the most operational value. "How long must we photograph good parts before this is useful?" determines whether the approach is viable for short production runs, and it is the question the published literature almost never addresses.
