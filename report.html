Finding Defects Without Examples

How do you build an inspector that catches faults it has never seen? And what happens when you measure it honestly — including the four times our own conclusions turned out to be wrong.

01

The problem, and why it isn't classification

A factory line makes parts. Occasionally one comes out wrong — a scratch, a dent, a missing wire, a smear of contamination. You want a camera to catch those before they ship.

The obvious approach is to train a classifier: show it good parts, show it bad parts, let it learn the difference. That approach fails immediately, and understanding why is the foundation for everything else here.

In plain terms

You can photograph a thousand good parts in an afternoon. Collecting a thousand defective parts might take a year — and the moment a supplier changes or a tool wears down, a brand-new kind of defect appears that was in none of your photos.

This is a fundamental asymmetry. "Normal" is a compact, densely sampled thing — good parts all look much alike, and you have thousands. "Defective" is unbounded — there are infinitely many ways to be wrong, and you can never enumerate them.

So the problem gets reframed. Instead of learning the boundary between two classes, you describe what normal looks like, and flag anything that sits far enough outside that description. In the literature this is one-class modelling or out-of-distribution detection.

The consequence that surprises people

There is no loss function. There are no epochs. Nothing is trained. A loss function measures how wrong a prediction was, so it needs a right answer to compare against. Here there are no labelled defects to be wrong about. There is nothing to backpropagate.

If nothing is trained, where does the intelligence come from? From a network someone else trained years ago, on a completely different task, which we use frozen and never modify. Section 3 explains why that works.

02

The dataset: MVTec AD

Published in 2019 by MVTec Software GmbH, a Munich company that sells industrial machine-vision systems. That origin matters — it was built by people who sell inspection equipment, to answer a question their customers kept asking.

3,629training images — every one defect-free, by design
1,725test images, good and defective mixed
15categories: 5 textures, 10 objects
~73distinct defect types across them

Four design decisions worth understanding, because each one shapes what the results can and cannot tell you.

The defects were made on purpose

Researchers physically damaged real objects — scratched, bent, dented, contaminated them. They did not wait for a factory to produce failures, because that would have taken years. This gives controlled coverage of failure modes, but staged damage may not match how parts actually fail in service.

Training data is defect-free by construction

This mirrors the real constraint rather than a convenient split. It is also an assumption worth checking rather than trusting: if even a few defective images leak into training, the model learns that defects are normal, scores collapse, and you get a plausible-looking number that measures nothing.

Every defect ships with a pixel-precise mask

So you can evaluate not just whether a part is bad but where. We have not used these yet — it is the largest remaining gap in this work.

Acquisition is controlled — and some categories rotate

Fixed lighting, roughly centred objects. But screw, hazelnut and metal_nut are randomly rotated on purpose, to test rotation robustness. Remember screw — it becomes the villain of this report.

Licence, because it matters commercially

MVTec AD is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — non-commercial research only. It legally cannot ship inside a product. What transfers to industry is the method, validated on a benchmark everyone shares.

03

Features: borrowed intelligence

A digital image is a grid of brightness numbers. Comparing two images by subtracting those numbers directly is almost useless — shift a photo one pixel to the left and every number changes, though nothing meaningful did.

What you want is a representation: a way of turning an image into a list of numbers where distance means visual difference. That is what a pretrained neural network gives you.

In plain terms

A network trained to recognise a thousand everyday objects had to learn, on the way, what edges, textures, and materials look like. We throw away its final answer ("this is a dog") and keep its intermediate impressions — which turn out to be an excellent general-purpose description of surfaces. These are called features.

What is AUROC?

Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve. A single number from 0 to 1 measuring how well a score ranks defective items above good ones.

Concretely: pick one random defective part and one random good part. AUROC is the probability the defective one gets the higher anomaly score. 1.0 means perfect ranking; 0.5 means the score is useless; below 0.5 means it is actively backwards.

The critical property — and a theme of this whole report — is that AUROC is threshold-free. It measures ranking, not decisions. A factory has to actually decide "ship or reject", and Section 6 shows those two things disagree badly.

04

The method, step by step

The algorithm is called PatchCore. It has four moving parts, and no training anywhere.

1 · Keep the grid, don't average it

A naive approach squashes the whole image into one list of numbers. That destroys small defects: a 20-pixel scratch on a 700-pixel screw barely moves the average, so the image lands right back in the middle of "normal".

Instead we keep a grid — a 224-pixel image becomes a 28×28 grid, so 784 separate descriptions of 784 small regions. This single change is what makes the method work.

2 · Let each patch peek at its neighbours

Each patch description is averaged with the eight around it. This widens what each one "sees" without making the grid coarser — you normally trade those against each other, and this buys a little of one without paying in the other.

3 · Build a memory bank, then shrink it

Store every patch from every good training image. That is hundreds of thousands of vectors per category — too slow to search for every part coming down a line. So keep a coreset: a subset chosen by repeatedly adding whichever patch is furthest from everything already picked.

Why furthest, not random?

Because we care about the outline of normal, not its centre. The decision boundary lives at the edges. Random sampling would keep the crowded middle and lose the rare-but-perfectly-normal patches — the unusual-looking good parts that, if forgotten, become false alarms.

4 · Score by distance, take the worst patch

For a new part: every patch measures its distance to the nearest patch in the memory bank. The part's score is the maximum over its patches — one bad region is enough to condemn a part. Averaging would dilute a single anomalous patch across 783 innocent ones, which is the arithmetic opposite of what inspection means.

A common misconception

This is not finding the peak of a probability distribution. Nothing here estimates a density. The score is literally "how far is this from the nearest good thing I have ever seen" — a question about the boundary of normal, not its centre. That is also why normal can be lumpy and multi-shaped (a screw at 0° and one at 90° are both fine) without breaking anything.

05

The threshold: where scores become decisions

Everything so far produces a number per part. A factory needs a verdict. Somewhere you draw a line: above it, reject.

You cannot tune that line against defects — you have none. So you set it from good parts alone: score a batch of known-good items and put the line at some percentile of those scores. The 99th percentile means "accept the most unusual 1% of genuinely good parts being flagged".

Two kinds of mistake, and they are not equal:

Escapea defective part passes inspection and reaches a customer. Expensive.
False alarma good part gets flagged and needs a human look. Cheap.

06

Results, and four times we were wrong

This is the part worth reading. Each finding below was overturned by the next experiment — which is what measuring carefully actually looks like.

Experiment 2

"DINOv2 is much worse than a CNN"

A modern self-supervised transformer scored 0.735 on screw against the old CNN's 0.955. Conclusion: newer backbone, worse results.

Experiment 3 — overturned

Two things had changed at once

Switching CNN→transformer also changed the grid from 28×28 to 16×16. Matched on resolution, the transformer won. The comparison had a confound: two variables moved, and we credited the wrong one.

Experiment 4 — overturned again

The winning margin was luck

Re-running with five different random starts: the "32% better" arm was really 3.6% better, and the ranking of four arms was in the wrong order. One value we had published was the worst of five draws.

Experiment 6 — overturned a third time

Every comparison used a broken threshold

The 99th percentile was 15× worse than simply using the median. Re-ranked at a sane threshold, the arm we had dismissed as a poor trade came first.

The last check — the whole metric was wrong

Rejecting every part beats our best model

MVTec's test set is 73% defective. A real line is under 2%. On that inverted balance, a policy of scrapping everything unexamined costs 467 — and our best detector costs 559.

Experiment 8 — a fourth reversal

The newest backbone is the wrong one

DINOv3, released after DINOv2 and better on every public benchmark, placed fifth of eight — behind a 2015 WideResNet. Checked twice, once holding the patch grid fixed and once holding input size fixed, because those cannot both be matched across the two models. DINOv2 won both.

A hardware assumption, measured

The GPU was idle 85% of the time

We were about to buy a card three times faster. Sampling utilisation showed 0% in twelve of fourteen readings, and 764 MiB used of 20 GB. The bottleneck was one CPU core decoding PNGs while 128 sat idle. Threading it was 9.8× faster and provably bit-identical — and the faster GPU would have idled just the same.

And the measuring instrument itself

The new metric was broken

Our first pixel-level score came out 16× below a published baseline, using a better configuration than that baseline. Rather than believe it, we fed the metric predictors whose answer is known by construction. A perfect detector scored 0.879 instead of 1.0 — the integral stopped short of its own limit.

The headline result: how little data is enough?

This is the question the published literature does not answer, and the one a plant commissioning a new product actually asks. How many photographs of good parts before the inspector works?

10median images to reach 99% of full-data performance
2enough for four categories to exceed 0.999
256and screw never gets there at all
What this means on a factory floor

At a few seconds per photograph, ten images is about a minute of production. Even the hardest well-behaved category is under an hour. The answer to "two hours or two weeks?" is minutes — except when it isn't, and the spread is the finding. A single number would have been the wrong answer.

07

Three knobs, and what each one really controls

Resolution has a knee

How finely you chop the image into patches matters enormously — up to a point, then it reverses.

changeAUROCcostverdict
16×16 → 28×28 grid+0.027 −42%transformative
28×28 → 40×40 grid+0.004 +13%costs more, gains nothing

Below the knee, resolution is the dominant variable. Above it you are paying for patches that no longer separate anything.

A newer backbone is not automatically a better one

DINOv3 is the successor to DINOv2 — larger, newer, better on public benchmarks. On this task it placed fifth of eight, behind a WideResNet from 2015.

comparisonDINOv2DINOv3winner
grid matched (28×28)0.9786 0.9744v2 by 0.0042
input matched (448 px)0.9813 0.9744v2 by 0.0069

Two framings, because a patch-14 and a patch-16 model cannot match both grid and input size. The margin widens when input is held fixed, so this is not a resolution artefact.

Per category it is not a uniform loss, and that is the interesting part. DINOv3 wins the small structured objects — screw +0.016, capsule +0.015, transistor +0.013 — and loses the textures badly, wood −0.101. One category accounts for most of the gap. "DINOv3 is worse" really means "much worse at one thing, mildly better at four others".

Descriptor width is not the driver

Each patch is described by a list of numbers — 1536 of them for one backbone, 384 for another. Intuition says more is better. It is not even monotonic: a 768-number description beat the 1536 one, while the 384 one lost badly. What matters is descriptor quality, not quantity.

The memory bank is a stability knob, not an accuracy knob

We had used 1% of patches because the original paper does. Across a 125× range of bank sizes, total cost varied by only 7% — but run-to-run reproducibility improved 4.5×.

Why an engineer should care

If you have to certify an inspection system, a result that shifts when you rebuild it is a problem regardless of its average accuracy. Pick 1% for cost; pick 25% if the system has to give the same answer twice, and pay 25× the memory for it.

08

The finding that undoes the rest

Late in the work we checked something we should have checked first: what does the dumbest possible policy cost?

policyescapesfalse alarmstotal cost
Scrap every part unexamined0 467467
Our best detector (arm D, p20)— —559
Ship everything unexamined1,258 0125,800

A policy of throwing away every part, with no camera at all, beats our best model. Not because the model is bad — because the measurement is.

MVTec's test set is 73% defective. A real production line runs at well under 2%. On a benchmark where most parts are already broken, and escapes are priced at 100× a false alarm, indiscriminate rejection is close to optimal — and no detector can look good against it.

What this invalidates, and what survives

Every cost figure in this report is computed on an inverted class balance, which means the absolute costs are not meaningful and the "median beats 99th percentile" result is partly an artefact of it. At a realistic 1% defect rate the weighting flips almost exactly, false alarms start to dominate, and the optimum swings back toward a high threshold.

What survives untouched: every AUROC, the data-efficiency curves, the resolution knee, and the reproducibility findings. Those are all threshold-free or class-balance-free.

The fix is to reweight each error by the defect rate a real line would see, which is the next experiment queued.

09

The harder benchmark, and where it hurts

Everything above is MVTec AD, which is close to solved. Its successor, MVTec AD 2, was built because of that: 8 scenarios, images at 2448×2048, and defects deliberately chosen to be hard. Published methods sit below 60% on its headline metric, so unlike AD there is real room to move.

It also asks a question we had never asked. Everything so far answered is this part defective. AD 2 is scored on where — per-region overlap, which weights a one-pixel scratch the same as a defect covering a quarter of the frame.

First contact went badly, and instructively

Our first AD 2 score was 16× below a published baseline — while using a better feature configuration than that baseline. Rather than report it, we tested the measuring instrument, found a real bug, fixed it… and the number barely moved. The result was genuine.

Localisation doubled from 224 to 448 — expected, since we were downsampling 2448 px images to 224 and erasing the small defects the benchmark exists to test. Then it stopped. Doubling again to 768 gained nothing, and image AUROC hovers around 0.5 throughout — a coin flip.

That plateau is the informative part. If small defects were the whole problem, resolution would keep paying. It doesn't. So something else is dominating, and more pixels cannot reach it.

The obvious suspect was distribution shift. AD 2 deliberately varies lighting between training and test, and a method that flags whatever sits far from its training images has no defence: a perfectly good part under new lighting is far from them. That would drown the defect signal entirely.

So we tested it rather than asserting it — scoring three defect-free sets against the same memory bank and measuring how far each had drifted, in units of the training set's own spread.

The hypothesis was wrong, and the way it was wrong matters

Across all eight, defect signal beats lighting shift roughly 3 to 1. Distribution shift is not the general problem.

But can — the one scenario every number above was measured on — has 2.7σ of shift and zero defect signal. Good and defective parts score identically there. Of eight scenarios we had picked the single most unrepresentative one, alphabetically, and drawn a dataset-wide conclusion from it.

Four scenarios carry real signal — vial at 2.7σ, sheet_metal 1.9σ, walnuts 1.8σ. Three others have neither shift nor signal and are simply hard. One is a domain-gap problem wearing a defect-detection costume.

So the real figure is above the published baseline, not a catastrophe — and the diagnostic predicted the ordering before we ran it. The two strongest scenarios by measured defect signal are the two strongest by localisation.

One more disagreement, in a new place

sheet_metal breaks the pattern: strong image-level signal (1.9σ, AUROC 0.70) but almost no localisation (0.034). It has 1,539 defect regions — far more than any other scenario, most of them tiny. The model knows the part is bad and cannot say where.

Detectable is not localisable, which is the same lesson as ranking-is-not-deciding, arriving from a new direction.

Why this is the most useful thing on this page

A single diagnostic, needing no labels beyond the folder names, separated "the model can't see the defect" from "the model is looking at the wrong thing" — and showed they demand opposite fixes. More resolution would have helped the first group and done nothing for can, which is exactly the plateau we measured before understanding why.

10

What a junior engineer should take from this

  1. AUROC is not a decision. It measures ranking. Four separate times in this project, the model that ranked best made the worst decisions. Always report the operating point too.
  2. Change one thing at a time. Our CNN-vs-transformer comparison moved the backbone and the resolution, and we credited the wrong one for a whole experiment.
  3. Run it more than once. A single random seed put four configurations in the wrong order and made one look 32% better than it was.
  4. Check the trivial baseline first. If "reject everything" beats your model, the problem is your metric, not your model — and it is embarrassing to discover that at the end rather than the start.
  5. Report per class, never just the mean. The average hid that one backbone wins on textures and loses on small parts. There is no universal best; the right choice is category-dependent.
  6. Write down predictions before running. One of ours was flatly wrong, and the wrongness was more informative than a confirmation would have been.

Still open

  • Pixel-level evaluation. The dataset ships masks; we have only ever asked "is this part bad", never "where".
  • Realistic class balance, per Section 8.
  • Diagnosing screw. It fails on every axis and we have never once looked at what it gets wrong.
  • MVTec AD 2 — the successor benchmark, where the best published methods are still under 60%, and results are verified by an evaluation server rather than self-reported.
